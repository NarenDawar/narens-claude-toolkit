# Build a writing skill around specificity, not blacklists

**A skill that makes Claude sound like a real person should be built around three durable writing principles (be specific, commit to a claim, write for one reader), supported by a short, dated, structure-first catalog of tells and a deterministic lint script. It should not be built as a banned-word list or a detector-evasion tool.** The reasons come from the evidence. Word-level tells are measured but decay as models change; structural and discourse-level tells last longer but are mostly community-observed rather than measured. Phrase bans are documented to backfire in practice. The best AI detectors are strong on clean, unedited, medium-to-long text, but they say nothing about quality, are weak on short, edited or ESL text, and keep changing. The strongest human judges are heavy LLM users, and they flag generic, padded, vague prose, which is a quality defect. The best-evidenced lever for sounding like a particular person is giving the model two to five real samples of that person's writing. Existing "humanizer" skills (blader/humanizer, stop-slop and others) are popular but have no controlled evaluation, and they are almost all post-hoc rewriters, a gap a pre-generation "write mode" and a measurement harness can fill. Text watermarks are statistical word-choice signals, not hidden characters, and no cleanup touches them. A new skill should say so plainly and should never promise a detector score. Evidence labels used below: **measured** (quantified in a study), **documented** (stated in a primary or official source), **anecdotal** (user reports, blogs, single experiments), **unverified** (reported secondhand or not confirmable from the notes).

## Word-level tells are measured but perishable; structure lasts longer

The best word-level data comes from one corpus. Kobak et al. analysed 15M+ PubMed abstracts from 2010 to 2024 and found that "delves" had an excess ratio of **28.0**, "underscores" 13.8 and "showcasing" 10.7. 2024 had **454 excess words** against 190 in 2021, and **66% of the excess style words were verbs** ([measured; arXiv v5](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5)). Ratios differ across preprint versions (an earlier version gave underscores 10.9 and showcasing 10.2), so any list should name the version it cites ([arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v2)). Two caveats limit what a skill can do with this. All of the measured data is scientific abstracts and peer reviews; transfer to blogs, email, marketing or fiction is unmeasured in the notes. And the authors cannot separate direct LLM use from human adoption of LLM-preferred words. Corroboration is thinner: Liang et al. estimated 6.5% to 16.9% of peer-review text was substantially LLM-modified, but that is a distribution estimate with no confirmed per-word list ([measured; arXiv 2403.07183](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07183v1)). Juzek and Ward found 21 focal words and found results consistent with RLHF playing a role ([measured; arXiv 2412.11385](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.11385)).

Grammar-level tells also have measurements. Reinhart et al. (PNAS 2025) report instruction-tuned models using present participial clauses at roughly 2 to 5 times the human rate and nominalizations at about 1.5 to 2 times, with GPT-4o using agentless passives at about half the human rate. The figures reached the notes through secondary summaries rather than the paper, and a tweet-corpus replication shrank the gaps ([measured via secondary; CMU summary](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/news/news-stories/2025/large-language-models-writing-text)). This supports a lint rule for trailing "-ing" analysis clauses and zombie nouns, with thresholds treated as relative to a baseline, not absolute.

Everything above the sentence level comes mostly from Wikipedia's WikiProject AI Cleanup guide, which is a volunteer field guide, not a study. Its catalog covers inflated significance ("stands as", "a testament to"), promotional tone ("nestled", "boasts"), vague attribution to unnamed "experts", tacked-on participial analysis, copula avoidance ("serves as" for "is"), negative parallelism ("not just X but Y"), formulaic "Despite its X, faces challenges" endings, title-case headings, bold overuse, inline-header bullet lists, and chatbot residue like "Certainly!" and "I hope this helps" ([community consensus; Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)). The page ties vocabulary to model eras (2023 to mid-2024 "delve", "tapestry"; mid-2024 to mid-2025 "fostering", "highlighting"; later "emphasizing", "showcasing"), and says "delve" declined from late 2024. The era table is community judgement, and its newest entry came from a fetch summary only, so it needs checking against the live page. The page also lists what is not evidence: perfect grammar, formal prose, a lone "Additionally", curly quotes (which Claude and Gemini typically do not produce anyway), and "not just X" in human listicles. Folk items such as tricolon overuse, "Let's dive in" and fake-profound closers matched no measured source in the notes and stay **unverified**.

The inference that follows from this: vocabulary is the most measurable and least durable layer, and the structural layer is the reverse. blader/humanizer v3.1 encodes the same conclusion by ranking structural patterns first and stating that word habits change per release while structural ones persist ([documented in the repo's own text; raw SKILL.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/blader/humanizer/main/SKILL.md)). Human style is also converging on LLM style, which cuts both ways. A Substack count of 1,276 essays found em dashes rising from about 1 to nearly 5 per 1,000 words from 2021 to April 2026, though the sample is small and the author concedes it cannot separate human adoption from AI authorship ([anecdotal; Substack](https://mediumismessage.substack.com/p/ai-generated-substacks-em-dashes)). A single tell is weak evidence; combinations are stronger; and no tell is proof.

## Claude's own habits are version-specific, and the quantitative evidence is thin

The best-documented Claude-specific tell is sycophantic agreement. GitHub issue #3382 (opened 12 July 2025, Claude Code v1.0.51) reports "You're absolutely right!" appearing on "a sizeable fraction of responses," drew about 180 comments and was locked in September 2025. It also records that CLAUDE.md rules forbidding the phrase did not stop it ([anecdotal but high volume; GitHub](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code/issues/3382)). No measured rate exists, and whether it persists in the 5.x family is **unverified**.

Anthropic's own documents confirm the habits indirectly, and they are the strongest evidence in this area ([documented]). Published claude.ai system prompts tell Claude to use minimum formatting, prose over bullets for reports and explanations, no emojis or asterisk actions, at most one clarifying question, and not to rewrite a whole piece when asked to change one thing ([Opus 5.5 system prompt](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/release-notes/system-prompts/claude-opus-5-5)). Because a system prompt is a patch layer, each rule likely maps to a base-model tendency (an inference), which means API use without a system prompt exposes more of them. The prompting guide says the latest models are "more direct and grounded," "less machine-like" and less verbose ([prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)), but the 5 family diverges: Opus 5 runs longer, narrates what it is about to do and writes longer files ([Opus 5 guide](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-opus-5)). Fable 5.1 uses bold and headers less, writes denser prose with longer sentences and fewer paragraph breaks, sometimes describes next steps instead of doing them, and can produce "mannered prose" that swaps direct statement for metaphor ("a dial worth turning" for "a parameter worth varying") ([Fable 5.1 guide](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-fable-5-1)). So the skill should not assume one Claude voice. Bullet-and-bold output is an older-model tell, density and metaphor are a Fable 5.1 risk, and length and narration are an Opus 5 risk.

The only peer-reviewed Claude stylometry (Sun et al., ICML 2025) is a measured result on 2024-2025 chat APIs: Claude favors "here," "according to," "based on the text," uses little bold or headers, and is "more concise and direct," while a classifier separated five model families at 97.1% accuracy ([measured; arXiv 2502.12150](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.12150v2)). That sits awkwardly with the folk image of heavily formatted Claude output, which fits older chat-UI behavior better. Other Claude-attributed claims are blog-level: em dashes (Upstatement), a banned em dash being replaced by another repetitive habit (Will Francis), and parenthetical asides as a top flagged pattern in a PCWorld test, which measures what Claude flags rather than what it writes ([anecdotal](https://willfrancis.com/how-to-stop-claude-writing-like-an-ai/)). No study measures "I'd be happy to," "genuinely," "honestly" or bold-lead bullets for Claude. Independent comparisons of Claude prose are also compromised: EQ-Bench Creative Writing uses Claude models as judges and does not control self-preference, and its slop score is a side measure outside the Elo ([documented; EQ-Bench](https://eqbench.com/about.html)). Claude's high ranking there says little about whether human readers find it less machine-like.

Anthropic's steering advice is consistent across its docs and shapes the skill's design. Tell Claude what to do instead of what not to do, match the prompt's own style to the output you want, and use positive examples; for mannered prose, define the anti-pattern with contrast pairs and say "when a literal phrase is available, use it" ([documented](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-fable-5-1)). The claim that bans cause substitute phrases rests on blogs and general inference; no Anthropic statement says it. Prefilled assistant turns, the old way to kill preambles, return a 400 error from Claude 4.6 onward, so openers must be controlled by instruction ([documented](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)).

## Watermarks and invisible characters are separate problems that cleanup cannot merge

The watermark picture changed in August to October 2026. Anthropic announced (14 August, updated 1 September) that future Claude models carry a text watermark built on the SynthID-Text approach; it says "Nothing is added to the text and there are no hidden characters," that the mark cannot identify a person or chat, that it shows only that Claude was "likely involved," and that it cannot distinguish "Claude wrote this" from "Claude heavily edited this." Detection is a private-preview API for approved groups, and models launched before 2 August 2026 get a transition period ([documented; Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-text-watermark)). OpenAI announced on 5 October 2026 an invisible text watermark that is on by default for EU ChatGPT and Codex users (opt-in for API customers), with a reported detection rate of about 80% at 200 tokens and 95% at 400 tokens at 1% FPR, falling from about 92% to 66% when 10% of words are replaced by synonyms. These numbers come from secondary coverage because OpenAI's page was not loadable ([documented via secondary; Nemko Digital](https://digital.nemko.com/news/ai-text-watermarking-openai-sets-eu-rollout)). Google's Gemini app has used SynthID-Text since 2024 ([documented; Nature paper copy](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11499265/)), but whether Gemini API text is marked is **unresolved**: a forum user first said no, then said yes, citing nothing ([anecdotal; Google AI forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/does-gemini-api-text-output-carry-synthid-watermarking-gemini-2-5-flash-lite-gemini-3-1-flash-lite-eu-ai-act-art-50-2/177241)). Older advice that "OpenAI doesn't watermark" is now stale.

Robustness is partial and has been attacked. Light edits probably survive and a full word-by-word rewrite removes the mark, per Anthropic, and Google says confidence drops with heavy rewriting or translation ([documented](https://ai.google.dev/responsible/docs/safeguards/synthid)). Recursive paraphrasing cut detection on roughly 300-token text from 99.3% to 9.7% TPR at 1% FPR ([measured; Sadasivan et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11156v4)), and "watermark stealing" enabled spoofing on schemes considered safe ([measured; Jovanovic et al.](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2402.19361)). Spoofing means a detected mark is not proof of authorship. No independent evaluation of Anthropic's or OpenAI's 2026 schemes exists in the notes.

Invisible-character artifacts are a different thing, and conflating them is a common error. Real, documented items include the narrow no-break space U+202F in OpenAI reasoning-model output (April 2025), which recurred in GPT-5 non-reasoning output as a rendering bug ([anecdotal but user-reproduced; OpenAI community](https://community.openai.com/t/gpt-5-non-reasoning-outputs-u-202f-narrow-no-break-space-instead-of-normal-spaces-breaks-text-rendering-on-macos-apps/1362321)); leaked citation markers such as `::contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}` ([anecdotal; OpenAI community](https://community.openai.com/t/why-is-chatgpt-adding-content-references/1076836)); `turn0search0` tokens wrapped in private-use characters U+E200 to U+E202 ([anecdotal](https://community.openai.com/t/streamed-web-search-citations-leaking-citation-markers-into-text-output/1390157)); and `utm_source=chatgpt.com` link parameters from June 2025, reportedly dropped around October 2026 without OpenAI confirmation ([documented, then unconfirmed; PPC Land](https://ppc.land/chatgpt-adds-utm-parameters-to-more-links-for-better-analytics-tracking/)). Unicode tag characters (U+E0000 to E007F) are a prompt-injection vector, not a watermark ([documented; Cisco](https://blogs.cisco.com/ai/understanding-and-mitigating-unicode-tag-prompt-injection)). The claim that ChatGPT inserts zero-width spaces as a watermark has no primary support (**unverified**), and the claim that OpenAI called U+202F a "quirk of reinforcement learning" is likewise secondhand. The C2PA text method does embed manifests in invisible variation selectors, but only third-party implementations were found and no major chatbot is reported to use it ([documented; Unicode L2/26-042](https://www.unicode.org/L2/L2026/26042-embedded-metadata-in-plain-text.pdf)). The legal layer (EU AI Act Article 50 applying from 2 August 2026, with a public-interest-text disclosure duty that carries an editorial-responsibility carve-out) comes from law-firm summaries; the statute was not read ([documented via secondary; Jones Day](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2026/06/european-commission-publishes-final-code-of-practice-on-marking-and-labelling-aigenerated-content)). The skill should not give legal advice.

## Detectors measure resemblance and judges measure genericness; neither is a quality score

Detector evidence splits sharply by year and by who ran the test. In 2023, seven GPT detectors flagged TOEFL essays by non-native writers at an average **61.2%** false-positive rate while passing US-student essays, and asking ChatGPT to enrich the vocabulary cut that to about 12%, which shows the scores respond to lexical statistics rather than authorship ([measured; Liang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.02819v3)). Twelve public tools plus Turnitin and PlagiarismCheck were judged "neither accurate nor reliable" in Weber-Wulff et al. (the specific summary figures were not checked against the paper) ([measured, partly unverified; arXiv 2306.15666](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15666v1)), and paraphrasing with DIPPER dropped DetectGPT's accuracy at 1% FPR from 70.3% to 4.6% ([measured; Krishna et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.13408)). By 2025 the best commercial classifiers looked much better in the lab. In Russell, Karpinska and Iyyer (ACL 2025, 300 non-fiction articles) Pangram reached 98.0% TPR at 2.0% FPR and GPTZero 85.3% at 0.7%, while Binoculars and RADAR were weak; on o1-Pro "humanized" output Pangram kept 90.0% TPR against GPTZero's 46.7% ([measured; arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.15654v2)). A Chicago Booth working paper (about 1,992 human passages across six genres, not peer reviewed) found Pangram's FPR essentially zero and the only tool holding a 0.5% FPR cap, with all commercial tools degrading under 50 words ([measured; Booth Review](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/2025/december/do-ai-detectors-work-well-enough-trust)). Two cautions apply: Pangram is a vendor with ties to this literature, and independent 2026 replications, especially on ESL, short or mixed human-AI text, were not found. Vendor figures (Winston 99.98%, Copyleaks 99.1%, Pangram 0% ESL FPR) run higher than independent ones and come mostly through review sites, so they are low-confidence. GPTZero's reply to Pangram's ESL claim gave a 7.7% FPR on TOEFL, or 1.1% if "possible AI" counts as negative.

Base rates matter more than headline accuracy. Vanderbilt computed that Turnitin's claimed 1% rate implied about 750 wrongly flagged papers among 75,000 and disabled the tool; Curtin switched Turnitin's detector off from 1 January 2026 ([documented; Vanderbilt](https://www.vanderbilt.edu/brightspace/2023/08/16/guidance-on-ai-detection-and-why-were-disabling-turnitins-ai-detector/); [Curtin](https://www.curtin.edu.au/news/oasis-news/update-on-turnitin-ai-detection-tool/)). Other university moves (Yale, Waterloo and others) were reported only secondhand.

Human judges show the largest spread in the evidence. Untrained readers were at chance on GPT-3 text (Clark et al. 2021), and 289 teachers could not reliably spot ChatGPT essays ([measured](https://aclanthology.org/2021.acl-long.565); [Leibniz-IPN](https://www.leibniz-ipn.de/en/research/publications/do-teachers-spot-ai-evaluating-the-detectability-of-ai-generated-texts-among-student-essays)). Yet in Russell et al., frequent-LLM-user experts reached 92.7% TPR at about 4% FPR individually, and a five-expert majority vote hit **99.3% TPR with 0% FPR**, staying at 100% on paraphrased and humanized text, while non-experts sat at 56.7% TPR and 51.7% FPR ([measured](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.15654v2)). Individual expert FPR ranged from 1% to 12%, and one expert fell to 0% TPR on humanized text. The cues they cited were vocabulary (53.1% of explanations), sentence structure (35.9%), originality (23.7%) and clarity (19.5%). This is the key bridge for the skill: the things skilled readers react to are generic, padded, vague prose, which is poor writing regardless of any score. Evasion advice also goes stale because detectors retrain against humanizers (Pangram's Humanizers variant and its DAMAGE paper are vendor-authored). The honest framing the notes support, which the skill should adopt, is that prose should be specific, accurate and voiced, that users should follow their venue's AI-disclosure rules, and that a low score does not certify human authorship nor a high one prove AI use.

## Existing humanizer skills offer good process designs and no proof they work

The category is large and unmeasured. blader/humanizer had **58,494 stars** and 4,635 forks on 19 October 2026 (created 18 January 2026, last push 16 October); hardikpandya/stop-slop had 18,157 stars but was seven months stale ([documented; GitHub API](https://api.github.com/repos/blader/humanizer)). Star counts grew from 33,700 in August to 49,188 in mid-September to 58,494 now, so they track distribution, not demonstrated effect. Their designs differ in useful ways. blader v3.1 (about 5,200 words in one SKILL.md) ranks 26 patterns strongest-first, justifies an edit on a single sighting only for the top five, runs a mark-draft-recheck workflow including a fact-and-number preservation check, lets a user's 2-3 paragraph writing sample override the pattern list (even its dash rule), and has an explicit "when not to act" section ([documented](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/blader/humanizer/main/SKILL.md)). Its README cites a blind test where judges preferred the rewrite 16 of 16 times, which the notes could not locate (**unverified**), and admits that detectors still flag most output. stop-slop is 68 lines plus three reference files with eight core rules and a 1-10 self-score (revise below 35/50), but its blanket bans (no adverbs, no passive, no em dashes) invite over-correction and its self-grading uses the same model that wrote the text ([documented](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/hardikpandya/stop-slop/main/SKILL.md)). slopkit pairs an editing skill with a replies-shaping skill and Node/Python gate scripts, and its README reports 99/100 against 65-84 for rivals while calling this a small-panel result ([vendor-reported](https://github.com/ehmo/slopkit)). isala404's `humanize` takes the opposite stance, "every output line must trace to the source," so it adds no voice ([documented](https://www.skills.sh/isala404/dotfiles/humanize)). Capital & Compute, which surveyed 30+ tools, argues that constraining the brief before generation works better than cleaning afterward, that nearly all popular tools rewrite finished text, and that regex checkers flag quoted buzzwords; it reports one tool with a published controlled evaluation that failed its own safety bar, which could not be independently located ([secondary, partly unverified](https://capitalandcompute.net/blog/anti-slop-ai-skills/)). The Agensi "~190 installs" figure in the original brief could not be reproduced (the listing showed 5-8 downloads).

Documented failure modes are mostly anecdotal but consistent: banning "not X, it's Y" yields "rather than X, Y" (the move survives, the string changes), a single Gemini experiment lost contrast-dependent concepts when the form was banned, and rule-of-three and negative parallelism have legitimate uses ([anecdotal; localskills.sh, Habr](https://habr.com/en/articles/1032620/)). The Antislop paper adds that plain token banning becomes unusable around 2,000 banned strings, a sampler-level result that does not transfer to a prompt-only skill ([measured; arXiv 2510.15061](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.15061v1)).

What does help has its own evidence ladder. Few-shot samples of a real author's writing beat zero-shot on style closeness across 400+ authors, though the gain is large for news and email (Enron email top-5 attribution 56.5-62.4% versus 27.1-28.9% zero-shot) and small for blogs and forums, 2 to 10 examples add little, and choosing examples by topic similarity hurt ([measured; arXiv 2509.14543](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.14543v1)). Style fidelity and detectability are separable: a separate study found human essays averaged perplexity 29.5 against 15.2 for matched LLM output even with few-shot ([measured; arXiv 2509.24930](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.24930)). Fine-tuning on one author's complete works reversed expert readers' preference for human writers and cut detector flags from 97% to about 3%, but this is a preprint using one fine-tuned model, and it is out of reach for a skill ([measured, preprint; arXiv 2510.13939](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.13939v4)). Human editing beat LLM editing in the Salesforce/CHI 2025 study of 1,057 paragraphs and a seven-category taxonomy of LLM idiosyncrasies ([measured; arXiv 2409.14509](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.14509)). Temperature barely changes novelty (one model, one prompt) ([measured, narrow; arXiv 2405.00492](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.00492v1)), and homogenization findings are contested: Moon et al. report each human essay adds more new ideas than each GPT-4 essay, while Doshi and Li report thematic convergence with structured stylistic differentiation ([contested; arXiv 2512.13697](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.13697v2)). Variance has to come from content, meaning real details and a stance, not sampling.

The craft canon maps neatly onto the tells, but only through secondary sources for the books. Orwell's rules (short words, cut words, active voice, no dead metaphors, and "break any rule" before writing something barbarous), Zinsser on clutter, and Pinker on the curse of knowledge and compulsive hedging all reduce to: be specific, commit, write for one reader ([documented for Orwell; secondary for the others](https://www.orwell.ru/library/essays/politics/english/e_polit)). Adverb and passive bans from King are contested judgment calls, not rules, so they should be defaults with exceptions. Practitioner skills supply the rest (rhythm, fragments, asides, self-corrections), and no citable source for those exists beyond the skill files themselves.

## Design recommendations: what goes where, what to measure, what not to claim

The layout follows Anthropic's skill guidance: SKILL.md under 500 lines, references one level deep with a contents list on long files, scripts executed rather than read, a third-person description with trigger phrases, evaluations written before documentation, and testing on Haiku, Sonnet and Opus ([documented; best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/agent-skills/best-practices)). The proposals are inferences from the evidence above, not tested designs.

| Component | Contents | Why |
|---|---|---|
| SKILL.md (about 150-250 lines, written in plain prose with minimal bold and bullets) | The three principles (specific, committed, one reader); two modes router (write mode, edit mode); a pre-generation brief (reader, purpose, stakes, the one point, real details available, voice sample); the voice-sample procedure; a "do not invent facts, quotes or numbers; ask for missing details" rule; "when not to act" (quotations, titles, pre-Nov-2022 text, code, legal text); how to run the lint script; the honest statement on detectors, watermarks and disclosure | Pre-generation guidance fills the gap Capital & Compute and the notes identify; a plain-prose skill follows Anthropic's "match prompt style to output" advice |
| references/tells-structural.md | Ranked, structure-first patterns (negative parallelism including split and reversed forms, inflated significance, vague attribution, participial tack-ons, forced triads, closers and summaries, assistant residue, "writing for the wrong reader"), each with a positive fix and a short before/after | Structure outlasts vocabulary; positive framing matches Anthropic's guidance and the substitution anecdotes |
| references/tells-lexical.md | Word and phrase list headed "as of 2026-10-19", labeled with source and evidence grade (Kobak measured, Wikipedia community) and noted as abstract-derived; a short backstop, not the core | Isolates the perishable layer so it can be refreshed per model release |
| references/claude-habits.md | Version-specific notes: sycophantic openers and closers, "Want me to...?" offers, Opus 5 narration and length, Fable 5.1 density and mannered prose with Anthropic's contrast pairs | Claude's habits differ by version; most are documented by Anthropic itself |
| references/genres/*.md | Blog, email/Slack, marketing, academic, fiction, with what flips (hedging is legitimate in academic text, the no-invention rule inverts in fiction, lead with the ask in email) | The notes hold that only a core of rules is universal and the rest is genre-dependent |
| references/voice-profile.md | Ask for 2-3 passages of the same genre as the task (not the same topic); extract sentence length mean and spread, contraction rate, punctuation habits, opening moves, person, hedging; write to the profile and check against it | Best-evidenced lever; casual blog voice is where imitation is weakest, so ask rather than guess |
| references/hygiene.md | Artifact cleanup: remove `oaicite`/`contentReference`/`turnNsearchN` tokens and U+E000-F8FF characters; map U+202F and U+00A0 to a plain space; strip `utm_*`; remove tag characters and unneeded zero-widths (keep ZWJ in emoji and ZWNJ in Persian/Indic text); say plainly this does nothing to statistical watermarks | Real, documented artifacts; correctness and cleanliness, not evasion |
| scripts/lint.py (stdlib only, advisory, verbose messages) | Counts per 1,000 words of em/en dashes, negative-parallelism variants, trailing "-ing" clauses, tricolons, bold-label bullets, repeated paragraph closers, listed lexical tells with their date; sentence-length mean and SD; paragraph-length variance; artifact characters; a before/after diff of numbers, names and quotes for edit mode. Compare against the voice profile where one exists instead of fixed thresholds | Deterministic checks beat self-grading; regex cannot read intent, so output is a report for judgment, not auto-fix |

The measurement plan should rest on human judgment, not detector scores. Run 20-30 prompts across genres comparing baseline Claude, the skill, blader and stop-slop; collect blind pairwise preferences from raters that include heavy LLM users (the sharpest readers in the evidence) and, where possible, the target author; track lint counts before and after; run a fact-preservation diff in edit mode; run a held-out voice-imitation test with two or three real samples scored by blind readers or an authorship classifier; test on Haiku, Sonnet and Opus; and re-run on each model release because tells drift. Avoid the same model grading its own output as the main metric (stop-slop's 35/50 is the weak form), and record detector scores only as a secondary, caveated number.

| Claim to avoid | Reason |
|---|---|
| "Passes AI detectors" or any promised score | Detectors are vendor-specific, retrain against humanizers, and are unvalidated as quality measures; fidelity and detectability are separable |
| "Removes watermarks" or "removes hidden characters, so undetectable" | Watermarks are statistical word-choice signals; only a full rewrite weakens them, and no guarantee exists; character hygiene is a different thing |
| Any single word, em dash or curly quote as proof of AI | Human adoption has risen and the Wikipedia guide lists these as ineffective indicators; individual expert FPR was up to 12% |
| Specific tell frequencies for Claude ("Claude says X in N% of replies") | No measured Claude phrase-frequency study exists; the best are one GitHub issue and one 2024-25 stylometry paper |
| "Em dashes are the AI tell" as a rule | Opt-out exists in ChatGPT, humans use them more, and bans produce replacement habits (anecdotal) |
| Absolute bans (no adverbs, no passive, no "not X but Y") | Contested craft advice, legitimate uses, and substitution failures |
| Delve/tapestry lists as current | The Wikipedia guide says "delve" declined from late 2024; keep any list dated |
| "Blind tests show X% preference" for blader or others | blader's 16-of-16 claim could not be located; no controlled evaluation of any humanizer exists in the notes |

The gaps are as consequential as the findings. No controlled study ties any humanizer skill, prompt list or "add personal detail" technique to human preference, and no study covers 2026-era models or lightly edited AI text at scale. There is no measured Claude-specific phrase frequency, no Claude numbers in the style-imitation studies, and no genre-by-genre tell-frequency data. Primary texts were not read for ICMJE and Nature policies, the EU Code of Practice, the PNAS paper, Weber-Wulff, or the craft books (Strunk and White, Zinsser, King, Pinker). Large parts of the Wikipedia page (about 154k of 254k characters in one note, and several Style and em-dash sections in another) were not read, and the "GPT-6" era entry is unconfirmed. Watermark facts for Meta, Mistral, xAI and open-weight models were not researched, and OpenAI's October 2026 announcement is known only from secondary sources.

## Conclusion

The research changes the framing of the task. "Sounding human" is not a property that a pass over finished text can add; the evidence says the readers who catch AI prose react to genericness, and genericness is cured by supplying what the model lacks: a particular reader, a stance, real details and a real voice sample. That makes the highest-value part of the skill the intake step (ask for the brief and the samples, and refuse to invent specifics) and the lowest-value part the word list, which is perishable, abstract-derived and likely to cause synonym substitution. A pre-generation mode with a lint script and a published, human-judged evaluation would already beat the field on the dimension that matters, since no popular competitor has published one.

The second implication is about honesty at the boundary. Watermarking is now deployed or announced by Anthropic, Google and OpenAI, detectors are good enough in the lab to matter and poor enough at the edges to harm, and expert human readers still catch humanized text. A skill that promises concealment would be selling something the evidence says cannot be guaranteed. A skill that promises specific, accurate, voiced prose, cleans real artifacts for correctness, and tells users to follow their venue's disclosure rules makes claims that survive each new model release and each new detector.
