# What changes by genre

Contents: universal rules, then email and chat, blog or essay, marketing, academic, fiction, and a note on speech.

Three rules hold everywhere. Do not invent facts, quotes or numbers. Cut throat-clearing and closers. Use named specifics. Almost everything else depends on the genre, so check the reader and the channel before applying any pattern from the tell lists.

## Email and chat (Slack, text, DMs)

- Lead with the ask or the answer. Put context after it.
- Contractions, fragments and plain words are right. Match how the sender actually writes to this person.
- Short. One request per message where possible, and a deadline if there is one.
- No "I hope this email finds you well", no closing offer.

## Blog post or personal essay

- Voice matters most here, and this is the genre where imitation from a sample is weakest, so ask for samples rather than guessing.
- First person and a real scene or example do the most work. Ask for them.
- Opinions, asides, and a mid-piece change of mind are fine. A tidy moral at the end usually is not.
- Headers and bullets only if the user normally writes with them.

## Marketing and product copy

- Concrete benefit with a number or a customer detail, not adjectives.
- Cut "seamless", "robust", "revolutionary", "unlock". Say what the product does on Tuesday morning.
- Three-item feature lists are acceptable when there are three features.
- Claims must be true and supportable. Do not invent testimonials or statistics.

## Academic and professional reports

- Hedging is legitimate. State the uncertainty precisely: what is unknown and why.
- Passive voice is sometimes right (the actor is irrelevant). Do not ban it.
- Formal register and perfect grammar are not tells. Do not degrade them to seem human.
- Never invent citations. If a source is needed and not supplied, leave a placeholder.
- Follow the venue's disclosure rules for AI assistance.

## Fiction

- The no-invention rule flips: the writer is inventing, so supply specifics that fit the story, but check continuity against what the user gave.
- Rhythm, subtext and concrete sensory detail matter more than any tell list. Dialogue should not explain feelings the scene already shows.
- Avoid generic names and settings. Ask for the story's details.

## Speech and scripts

- Written to be heard: shorter sentences, repetition on purpose, triplets can be right. Do not remove devices that work aloud.
